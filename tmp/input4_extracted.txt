Abstract: Purpose
To compare the predictive accuracy of four toric IOL formulas (Barrett, EVO, Hoffer®
QST, Kane) integrated in the ESCRS calculator. Barrett and EVO were tested with
measured (Barrett M and EVO M) and predicted (Barrett P and EVO P) posterior
corneal curvature.
Design
retrospective data analysis
Setting
multiple surgical centers in the USA
Methods
We analyzed 6753 eyes implanted with non-toric IOLs. Preoperative biometry,
including posterior corneal curvature, was obtained using the IOLMaster 700.
Predicted residual astigmatism was calculated via the proprietary websites of the four
toric formulas integrated into the ESCRS IOL Power Calculator. Primary endpoints
were the mean vector magnitude error and proportions of eyes with vector magnitude
prediction errors ≤0.50 D.
Results
Kane demonstrated the lowest mean vector magnitude prediction error (0.537 D),
significantly lower than EVO P (0.561 D), EVO M (0.57 D), Barrett M (0.579 D), Barrett
P (0.583 D), and Hoffer®QST (0.622 D) (all p<0.001). This result was consistent in
with-the-rule, against-the-rule, and oblique subgroups. Kane also achieved the highest
proportion of eyes with prediction error ≤0.50 D (p<0.001). For all formulas, centroid
values were significantly different from zero. The Kane formula showed the lowest total
variances, significantly lower than all other formulas (p<0.001).
Conclusions
The Kane formula showed statistically superior predictive accuracy with the lowest
errors and variances and the highest proportion of eyes within 0.50 D. However,
differences among formulas were small, and their clinical relevance for toric IOL
outcomes remains uncertain.

ABSTRACT  
Purpose: To compare the predictive accuracy of four toric IOL formulas (Barrett, EVO, 
Hoffer® QST, Kane) integrated in the ESCRS calculator. Barrett and EVO were tested with 
measured (Barrett M and EVO M) and predicted (Barrett P and EVO P) posterior corneal 
curvature.  
Design: retrospective data analysis 
Setting: multiple surgical centers in the USA 
Methods: We analyzed 6753 eyes implanted with non-toric IOLs. Preoperative biometry, 
including posterior corneal curvature, was obtained using the IOLMaster 700. Predicted 
residual astigmatism was calculated via the proprietary websites of the four toric formulas 
integrated into the ESCRS IOL Power Calculator. Primary endpoints were the mean vector 
magnitude error and proportions of eyes with vector magnitude prediction errors ≤0.50 D.  
Results: Kane demonstrated the lowest mean vector magnitude prediction error (0.537 D), 
significantly lower than EVO P (0.561 D), EVO M (0.57 D), Barrett M (0.579 D), Barrett P 
(0.583 D), and Hoffer®QST (0.622 D) (all p<0.001). This result was consistent in with-the-rule, 
against-the-rule, and oblique subgroups. Kane also achieved the highest proportion of eyes with 
prediction error ≤0.50 D (p<0.001). For all formulas, centroid values were significantly 
different from zero. The Kane formula showed the lowest total variances, significantly lower 
than all other formulas (p<0.001). 
Conclusions: The Kane formula showed statistically superior predictive accuracy with the 
lowest errors and variances and the highest proportion of eyes within 0.50 D. However, 
differences among formulas were small, and their clinical relevance for toric IOL outcomes 
remains uncertain.

 INTRODUCTION 
  Cataract surgery is increasingly regarded as a refractive procedure, with a strong 
emphasis on minimizing postoperative refractive error1. Up to 80% of patients achieve a 
residual spherical equivalent within ±0.5 diopters1. Nevertheless, up to 30-40% of eyes have  
pre-existing corneal astigmatism of 1 D or greater, which should be corrected during surgery to 
achieve a satisfactory visual outcome 2. Several techniques are available, including incisions 
along the steep axis, paired opposite clear-corneal incisions, limbal relaxing incisions, or 
implantation of a toric intraocular lens (IOL), the latter being the most predictable technique3.  
  Correcting preexisting corneal astigmatism poses unique challenges. Accurate and 
repeatable measurements of both anterior and posterior corneal curvature (PCC) are crucial; 
studies suggest that the repeatability of astigmatism measurements is only at the level of 0.4D4. 
Additionally, toric IOLs are often produced in 0.75 D increments, limiting the scope for fine-
tuning3. IOL rotational stability is another important factor. Finally, it can be difficult to 
accurately predict surgeon-induced astigmatism, partly because keratometry readings 
themselves tend to have low repeatability5, 6.  
  In recent years, methods for calculating toric IOL power have advanced substantially. 
One significant development was the Baylor Toric Nomogram, which accounts for the distinct 
impact of PCC on with-the-rule versus against-the-rule astigmatism7. Incorporating the Baylor 
Toric Nomogram into various formulas is suggested to improve predictions for residual 
cylinder8. Moreover, newer devices designed to measure PCC have enabled formulas that 
incorporate actual PCC measurements5. Nonetheless, studies comparing formulas that predict 
PCC with those that measure it directly have produced mixed results9, 10. 
  The ESCRS toric calculator is a relatively new tool designed to streamline workflows 
by enabling direct comparisons of residual cylinder predictions from four modern toric 
formulas: Barrett Toric, EVO Toric, Hoffer®QST Toric, and the Kane Toric formula. These 
formulas have been compared previously in a small dataset, the present study expands on that

MATERIALS AND METHODS  
  We included 6753 eyes that were collected by Zeiss (Germany) from the Veracity 
Surgical Planner database. Surgeries were performed from May 2020 to October 2024 across 
123 sites in United States. Only one eye per patient was analyzed. Preoperative biometry was 
performed with swept-source biometer IOL Master 700 (Zeiss, Germany).  Only eyes with 
keratometry (K), total keratometry (TK), and posterior keratometry (PK) measurements of 
successful quality were included.  None of the patients had undergone refractive surgery in the 
past or limbal relaxing incisions during the surgery. Postoperative follow-up took place at least 
three weeks after surgery, and only those eyes that achieved a postoperative manifest refraction 
and corrected distance visual acuity of 20/30 or better were included. Subjective refraction was 
converted to a 6-meter lane.  
  Since the current version of the ESCRS calculator does not support refractive 
predictions for EVO Toric and Barrett Toric IOLs using measured PCC, we accessed each 
calculator's proprietary website for this study. A Python script was used to input the data and 
retrieve the toric IOL predictions for monofocal lens implantation.  
  Statistical analysis followed the methodology described by Wang et al. and further 
refined by Koch et al12, 13. All calculations were performed in Excel (Microsoft, USA) and R 
Studio (Posit, USA) as per guidelines by Wang et al12.  
  Statistical analysis was performed for the entire dataset as well as for subgroups based 
on astigmatism orientation with-the-rule (WTR), against-the-rule (ATR) as and oblique. 
Orientation was classified according to anterior corneal astigmatism measured with the 
IOLMaster 700: (1) WTR: steep meridian 67.5°–112.5°; (2) oblique: 22.5°–67.5° or 112.5°–

of eyes implanted with the same IOL model. 
  For each formula, the prediction error of vector magnitude was calculated. 
Postoperative manifest astigmatism and the predicted residual astigmatism from each formula 
were first transformed into Cartesian coordinates (x- and y-components). The differences 
between the postoperative and preoperative components were then calculated. These 
differences were subsequently reconverted into polar notation, yielding a cylinder magnitude.  
The primary end-point of the study was the mean vector magnitude predictions error and 
percentage of eyes with vector magnitude prediction error ≤ 0.5D. The bootstrap-t method with 
Holm’s correction for multiple comparisons was applied. To evaluate the distribution of vector 
magnitudes, a table was created showing the percentage of eyes falling within predefined error 
intervals: ≤0.50 D, ≤0.75 D, and ≤ 1.00 D. For each interval, the percentage of eyes was 
reported. Comparisons between formulas were performed using McNemar’s Chi-squared test 
with continuity correction, appropriate for dependent data. Both nominal and Holm-adjusted p 
values were calculated for multiple comparisons.   
  To further assess vector direction and dispersion, we tested the null hypothesis that the 
bivariate mean and median of the whole dataset as well as dataset of subgroups do not differ 
from zero. Furthermore, total variances of x and y of vector components were compared 
between formulas14-16. Additionally, double-angle plots were included for each formula.

DISCUSSION  
  We found that the Kane formula demonstrated the highest accuracy in both scalar and 
vector analyses. The effect of incorporating PCC measurements varied by formula: it improved 
the performance of Barrett toric formula but not EVO toric formula. 
  Currently, two approaches are used to evaluate toric IOL outcomes: (1) analysis after 
toric IOL implantation, and (2) analysis after monofocal IOL implantation. While the 
monofocal approach (used in this study) may seem less reflective of real-world toric outcomes, 
it avoids confounding from postoperative IOL alignment and compensations for clinically 
irrelevant IOL rotation10, 11. Moreover, it facilitates the collection of larger datasets. To our 
knowledge, this is the largest study to date assessing the precision of modern toric IOL 
calculators. 
  Our results contrast with those of Liu et al., who reported the EVO formula as the most 
accurate and Kane as the least accurate among the toric formulas integrated into the ESCRS 
calculator. However, their dataset included only 85 eyes, and the analysis was performed after 
toric IOL implantation, introducing potential bias from suboptimal IOL positioning. 
Furthermore, the authors did not report results for EVO M formula11. Similarly, Yang et al., in 
a study of 146 eyes after toric IOL implantation, compared Barrett, EVO, Hoffer®QST, and 
Abulafia-Koch combined with Hill-RBF, and found EVO to be the most accurate, while Barrett 
with estimated PCC measurements was the least accurate. However, in this study authors did 
not measure PCC directly17.

 The role of PCC measurement in toric IOL calculations remains of particular interest to 
surgeons. Prior studies have yielded mixed results. A small study by Skrzypecki et al. found no 
significant difference between the Barrett toric calculator with and without PCC measurement, 
whereas Wang et al. reported a significant reduction in mean vector magnitude PE with 
measured PCC9, 10. Nevertheless, the result did not appear clinically relevant. Stewart et al. 
suggested that PCC measurement may be especially beneficial in eyes with non-vertical 
posterior corneal astigmatism. Other smaller studies also indicate that it may be useful in select 
cases, such as eyes with unusually steep posterior corneal surfaces19. 
  In our cohort, results support prior observations that the mean vector magnitude PE 
tends to hover around 0.5 D, with only ~50% of eyes achieving PE ≤ 0.5 D. Although inter-
formula differences reached statistical significance, they were not clinically meaningful, 
regardless of astigmatism orientation. This likely reflects the limitations of current corneal 
measurement technologies, where precision and repeatability may constrain outcomes more 
than the formulas themselves, as well as design of toric IOL, which are usually manufactured in 
0.5 D or 0.75 D steps.  
  One key limitation of our analysis is its retrospective nature. We relied on pre-existing, 
anonymized clinical data, meaning there was no opportunity to verify crucial aspects of each 
case or standardize data collection across centers. For example, our dataset included an eye 
with a average K value as low as 36.40 D, but we could not confirm whether this patient had 
undergone prior corneal refractive surgery; such unrecorded procedures could significantly 
influence postoperative outcomes. Likewise, the manifest refraction data were performed by 
different clinicians across multiple clinics, and we had no way to identify or evaluate individual 
techniques and inter‑observer variability. These factors collectively introduce potential sources 
of bias and heterogeneity that should be considered when interpreting our findings

In conclusion, this is the largest study to date comparing modern toric IOL formulas 
with and without PCC measurement. In our dataset of 6753 eyes, both scalar and vector 
analyses identified the Kane formula as statistically the most accurate. However, the 
differences between formulas do not appear to be clinically significant. 
 
What was known 
 The ESCRS toric calculator incorporates four contemporary formulas. 
 Previous studies have reported mixed results on whether incorporating posterior corneal 
curvature improves the accuracy of toric IOL power prediction. 
What this paper adds 
 This is the largest study to date comparing contemporary toric IOL formulas. 
 The Kane formula, which does not incorporate posterior corneal curvature 
measurements, demonstrated the highest accuracy. 
 While some formulas showed statistically significant differences, these were clinically 
small; improved outcomes in astigmatism correction might require advances in IOL 
design and measurement precision.
